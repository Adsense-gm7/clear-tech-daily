Push-Up Progressions: From Wall to Floor

By Raouf Rouissa · Updated October 21, 2026 · 4 min read

The push-up is a moving plank as much as it is a chest exercise. Using an elevated surface is not a shortcut; it is a measurable way to build the strength required for lower variations.

Safety note: This article provides general exercise education, not medical advice. Stop if you experience sharp pain, dizziness, chest pressure, or unusual shortness of breath.

Set the position

Place hands slightly wider than shoulder width. Spread the fingers, keep the wrists under or just behind the shoulders, and create a straight line from the head through the hips. Let the shoulder blades move naturally rather than pinching them together throughout the repetition.

Select your starting level

Begin at a wall, sturdy countertop, bench, or floor. Choose the height that allows 6–12 smooth repetitions while keeping the ribs and pelvis controlled. A higher surface reduces the percentage of body weight you press.

Perform each repetition

Lower the chest toward the surface while the elbows travel at a comfortable diagonal—not directly sideways. Stop before the shoulders roll forward or the hips sag, then press the surface away until the elbows are straight without forcefully locking them.

Progress without guessing

Complete three sets twice per week. Once you can perform 12 controlled repetitions in all sets for two sessions, use a slightly lower surface. Keep the previous level available for lighter days.

Shoulder-friendly adjustments

Try a slightly narrower hand position, a higher surface, or a neutral grip on stable handles. Pain that persists or worsens is a reason to stop and seek an assessment rather than repeatedly testing the same painful variation.

Your first practice session

Use a fixed countertop, then a lower fixed support. Check that it cannot move before leaning on it.

Suggested starting dose: 2–3 sets of 6–12. Treat this as a starting example for a generally healthy beginner, not an individual prescription. Rest until you can repeat the movement with control, and finish before technique breaks down.

A decision during your set

If you complete 12, 12 and 8 at one height, keep that height. Lowering it now would hide the difference between strength and fatigue.

Record support height, not just repetitions. A lower support changes the difficulty even when the repetition count stays the same.

Choose the next step

Wall → countertop → bench → floor. Stay at the current step until your repetitions or holds are repeatable. Progression is optional on a tired day; using an easier version can keep practice consistent.

Evidence behind the training framework

Currier and colleagues, 2023: resistance-training prescription systematic review and network meta-analysis. This review compares training approaches in healthy adults. It provides context for planning resistance training; it does not validate every technique cue or prescribe an individual starting load. The worked examples above are editorial applications, not experimental results.

Should I start on my knees or use an incline?

Both reduce the demand. An incline lets you practice a head-to-heel body line and gives you a measurable support height. A knees-down version is useful when a secure elevated support is unavailable. Choose the one you can repeat comfortably.

Keep a record of your exercise version, load, repetitions or hold time, and one technique observation.
