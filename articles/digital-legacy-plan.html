Create a Practical Digital Legacy Plan

Published September 17, 2026 · 8 minute read · By Clear Tech Daily Editorial Team

A digital legacy plan tells trusted people what exists, what matters, and which official process to follow—without leaving passwords exposed.

Create a category inventory

List categories rather than secrets: primary email, phone account, password manager, cloud storage, financial services, subscriptions, social media, websites, devices, and cryptocurrency if applicable. Note which accounts contain important files or ongoing charges.

Record your wishes

For each category, state whether you want data preserved, transferred, memorialized, or deleted. Identify photos, writing, domain names, or business records with long-term value. Separate personal wishes from legal authority; service rules and local law may control what another person can access.

Use official legacy tools

Some major services let you nominate a legacy contact or specify what happens after inactivity. Use those settings where available and review them after major account changes. Official tools are generally safer than sharing a password in an ordinary document.

Protect access information

Store recovery instructions in a secure place such as a password manager emergency-access feature, attorney-managed record, or sealed document. Tell the trusted person where instructions exist, not every credential. Keep multifactor recovery codes protected and current.

Coordinate with legal documents

Digital-property rules vary by state and service. Consider discussing important business, financial, or creative assets with a qualified estate-planning professional. This article is organizational education, not legal advice.

Do not include passwords in an unencrypted spreadsheet, email, or paper left in an obvious place. The plan should improve continuity without creating an easy path for theft.

Review annually

Remove closed accounts, add new important services, verify the trusted contact, and check that instructions can still be found. A one-page inventory updated yearly is more useful than a complex plan that becomes obsolete.
